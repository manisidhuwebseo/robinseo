Google's Shared AI Training Control in robots.txt: What Website Owners Need to Know

Google plans to support a shared AI training preference in robots.txt during 2027, giving publishers a common way to express content-use choices across participating AI providers. Here's what the proposal means for Google-Extended, Gemini grounding and website visibility.

By Robin SEOPublished: November 14, 2026AI Crawling & Technical SEO

Google has announced plans to support a shared AI training control in robots.txt, giving website owners a simpler way to express training preferences across participating AI providers. The announcement, published by Google Search Central on October 30, 2026, is based on work from the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) AI Preferences working group.

The important detail for site owners is the timeline: Google says it aims to support the shared control alongside other AI providers in 2027. As of November 14, 2026, this is a planned capability—not a live Google crawler setting that publishers should add to their files today.

What did Google announce?

At present, a website owner who wants to communicate different AI usage preferences may need to understand and maintain provider-specific controls. Google's proposal is to recognise a shared policy token in robots.txt, so one machine-readable preference can communicate a broad choice across AI providers that adopt the same signal.

Google says existing company-specific controls will continue to work alongside the shared signal. That means the common preference is intended to simplify broad choices, while provider-specific rules can still be used when a publisher wants to express a different preference for an individual service.

  • One shared signal: a common policy token can express an AI-training preference to participating providers.
  • Existing controls remain: provider-specific instructions such as Google's Google-Extended token are not being removed.
  • Planned timing: Google aims to support the control with other AI providers in 2027; it has not supplied a specific launch date.

How does the shared AI training control work?

The proposal is designed to use the familiar structure of a robots.txt file. The current IETF Internet-Draft gives __usage_ai-training as an example policy token. A draft example for expressing a site-wide preference against AI training looks like this:

User-agent: __usage_ai-training
Disallow: /

This is an illustration from the current IETF draft, not a ready-to-deploy instruction for every website. The draft is a work in progress and is not an adopted IETF standard; crawlers that do not implement the policy token may simply ignore that group. Google has said it plans to support the shared control in 2027, so publishers should wait for implementation guidance before adding or changing rules.

What is the difference between Google-Extended and Googlebot?

This is the distinction website owners should understand before making any robots.txt changes. Google's documentation describes Google-Extended as a standalone product token used to manage whether content Google crawls may be used to train future Gemini models and for grounding in Gemini Apps and Grounding with Google Search on Vertex AI.

Google-Extended is not the same as Googlebot. Google says Google-Extended does not affect a site's inclusion in Google Search and is not used as a Google Search ranking signal. Googlebot, by contrast, is the crawler associated with Google Search crawling and indexing.

Do not confuse AI training preferences with Search indexing controls. Blocking Googlebot can prevent Google from crawling pages you want to appear in Search; using Google-Extended is a separate control and does not, by itself, remove a page from Google Search.

Could the new signal affect visibility in Gemini?

Potentially, depending on the combination of preferences a publisher sets. Google says that when a website uses the shared policy token to opt out of AI training—and does not explicitly specify Google-Extended—Google plans to interpret that choice as if the publisher had disallowed Google-Extended.

That matters because Google-Extended currently covers both training uses and grounding in Gemini Apps. Google also says websites that specifically allow Google-Extended remain eligible to appear in Gemini Apps responses and receive clicks through grounding. In other words, the shared signal is not a promise of a separate, one-click switch for every possible AI use; publishers should review Google's final implementation details before choosing how to combine broad and Google-specific preferences.

None of this is an announcement of a new Google Search ranking factor. The distinction is about how content may be used by participating AI services, not a direct promise of better or worse traditional Search rankings.

What should website owners do now?

1. Review your current robots.txt rules

Check which rules apply to Googlebot and which apply to Google-Extended or other named crawlers. Make sure a broad disallow rule is not accidentally blocking pages you want Google to crawl. If you need help reviewing crawl access and indexability, see Robin SEO's technical SEO services.

2. Decide what you want to allow

Discuss AI training, AI answer grounding, content licensing and publisher preferences with the people responsible for your website and content. A publisher may have different priorities for public educational articles, original research, product information and licensed material. Record those decisions before changing crawler rules.

3. Keep Search crawling separate

Do not add a broad Googlebot block just because you are reviewing AI training preferences. If your goal is to remain eligible for Google Search, keep the Search crawler rules aligned with the pages you want indexed and check important URLs in Search Console after any technical change.

4. Wait for provider implementation guidance

Google's stated aim is 2027, and the underlying IETF document is still a draft. Monitor the Google Search Central announcement and the Google crawler documentation for confirmed support and final instructions.

5. Be especially careful with healthcare websites

Clinics should review only their public website content and the intended use of that content. Patient records, private appointment details and confidential documents should never be exposed on public URLs with the expectation that robots.txt will protect them. Keep sensitive information behind appropriate access controls and continue publishing accurate, useful patient-facing information.

Frequently asked questions

Is Google's shared AI training control live now?

No. Google announced its plan on October 30, 2026 and says it aims to support the shared control with other AI providers in 2027. No exact launch date has been announced.

What is the shared AI training token for robots.txt?

The current IETF Internet-Draft uses __usage_ai-training as its example policy token. It is a draft proposal, not a final standard or a guarantee that every AI crawler recognises the token today.

Does Google-Extended affect Google Search rankings?

Google says Google-Extended does not affect inclusion in Google Search and is not a ranking signal. Googlebot rules are separate and govern crawling for Search.

Will opting out of AI training remove my website from Gemini answers?

It may affect Google's use of content for Gemini grounding if the shared opt-out is used without an explicit Google-Extended instruction, because Google plans to treat that combination as a Google-Extended disallow. Review the final Google guidance before making a decision about training and grounding preferences.

Should a small business change its robots.txt file now?

Not solely because of this announcement. Review the rules already in place, document your preferences and wait until Google confirms support and publishes implementation guidance.

What this means for SEO and AI visibility

Google's shared AI training control is a meaningful development in how publishers may communicate content-use preferences across AI providers. It does not replace technical SEO, change Google's Search ranking rules or make a website more visible by itself. For now, the best response is to understand the difference between Googlebot and Google-Extended, keep existing Search access rules accurate and prepare to assess the final shared signal when it becomes supported.
