Google's CDN Guidance: How to Protect Googlebot During a Website Migration

Google's November 11 guidance explains why a cold CDN cache can increase origin requests—and why blanket bot challenges can interfere with Googlebot.

By Robin SEONovember 21, 2026Google Search NewsUSA & Canada

On November 11, 2026, Google Search Central published guidance titled “Moving to a CDN without overwhelming your origin (or blocking Googlebot)”. The article explains an operational problem that can appear when a site moves behind a content delivery network: crawler requests may reach the origin server more heavily during the period when edge caches are still cold.

The guidance matters to site owners, developers and SEO teams planning a CDN, DNS or hosting change. It is not a new ranking factor or an announcement of an algorithm update. It is practical advice about how to protect origin capacity while keeping verified search crawlers able to fetch public pages.

Why a new CDN can create an origin traffic spike

A CDN serves copies of eligible content from edge locations closer to visitors. Once an edge has a cached copy, repeat requests can be served without going back to the origin. But the first request for a particular URL at an edge is a cache miss: the edge must fetch the response from the origin before it can store and serve it.

Google explains that its crawling systems monitor host responsiveness. When a site responds quickly and consistently, crawl capacity can gradually increase when there is crawl demand. A new CDN may make responses faster, but crawlers systematically explore a broad set of URLs rather than only the handful of pages human visitors open most often. During cache warm-up, many of those first requests can still reach the origin.

Key distinction: a fast CDN edge does not automatically mean the origin is protected during the first crawl of every URL. Cache misses still have to be fetched and processed by the origin.

Why blanket bot protection can block Googlebot

Google warns that a blanket bot-protection rule can treat legitimate crawlers and unwanted automated traffic the same way. If a CDN or web application firewall responds to Googlebot with a CAPTCHA, browser challenge or other interstitial page, the crawler may not receive the page content it needs.

Google says that if verified crawlers remain blocked, its systems may back off and affected pages or other crawl-dependent features may become less available in Search. A site owner should therefore test security rules against verified crawler traffic rather than assuming every automated request is harmful.

Google's practical recommendations

1. Allow verified crawlers through the CDN and firewall

If bot filtering is needed, use the CDN or WAF's verified-bot controls where available. Google recommends identifying its crawlers through its published IP ranges and avoiding blanket challenges for verified crawlers. Another option is to target specific unwanted bots or clients rather than blocking all automated requests.

2. Pre-warm important caches at a controlled pace

Before routing all traffic through a new CDN, fetch high-priority pages and frequently crawled URLs through the edge network at a controlled pace. This gives the CDN an opportunity to populate caches before a broad wave of visitors and crawlers arrives. Prioritise key landing pages, important service pages, product or category URLs and pages expected to receive seasonal traffic.

3. Use temporary 429 or 503 responses only when necessary

If crawl traffic is putting critical load on the origin while caches warm up, Google's post says a temporary 429 (Too Many Requests) or 503 (Service Unavailable) response can act as a brake. The guidance describes a short window—hours or at most one to two days—not a permanent way to manage routine crawler traffic. Follow Google's separate guidance on urgently reducing crawl rate before applying such a measure.

A safe CDN migration checklist for SEO teams

  • Record important URLs, crawl patterns, response codes and baseline server performance before the move.
  • Confirm that HTML, CSS, JavaScript, images and other public assets are cached according to their intended rules.
  • Pre-warm priority URLs through the CDN without overwhelming the origin.
  • Verify Googlebot using Google's published verification guidance and IP ranges.
  • Test that WAF rules do not present CAPTCHA or browser challenges to verified crawlers.
  • Check robots.txt, canonical tags, redirects, sitemap access and representative page rendering after DNS changes.
  • Monitor cache-hit ratio, origin request volume, latency, 4xx/5xx responses and crawl activity during the transition.
  • Remove temporary rate limits and migration rules when the origin and edge cache are stable.

What this means for small businesses and healthcare websites

Most small websites do not need to change their CDN or security settings just because this guidance was published. It becomes relevant when a site is actively moving to a CDN, changing firewall rules or investigating unexplained crawl failures after an infrastructure change.

For clinics and other high-trust businesses, protect the accessibility of public service information and contact pages while keeping private systems appropriately restricted. Public marketing pages and authenticated patient portals have different access requirements; do not weaken protections on private systems to make public pages crawlable.

After a migration, review Search Console's crawl and indexing reports alongside CDN logs. Look for blocked responses, unexpected redirect chains, inconsistent cache behaviour and pages returning a challenge instead of content. Separate crawler verification from user-agent strings alone: user-agent text can be spoofed, so use the verification method recommended by Google and your CDN provider.

Frequently asked questions

Does Google's CDN guidance mean a CDN improves rankings?

No. The article is operational guidance about caching, origin load and crawler access. A CDN may improve delivery performance, but Google did not announce a new ranking factor in this post.

Why can Googlebot traffic increase after a CDN migration?

Faster, stable responses can signal that a host can support more crawling when demand exists. During cache warm-up, requests for uncached URLs can still pass through the edge to the origin.

Should every website whitelist all bots?

No. Site owners should distinguish verified crawlers they want to allow from unwanted automated clients. Use verified-bot controls and targeted rules rather than disabling security protections indiscriminately.

Can a site use 429 or 503 to control crawl load?

Google describes these as temporary responses when the origin is under critical load during a transition. They should not become a long-term substitute for appropriate caching and rate-management design.

What should be checked after moving behind a CDN?

Check page responses, cache behaviour, redirects, robots.txt, canonicals, sitemap access, WAF challenges, origin load and Search Console crawl/indexing reports. Test representative pages from outside the authenticated site environment.
